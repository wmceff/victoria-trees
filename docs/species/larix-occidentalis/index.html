Western larch Larix occidentalis

Native to the inland mountains of southeastern British Columbia and the northwestern United States.

Wildlife: Pine siskins eat its seeds in winter. Woodpeckers dig holes in the rotting heartwood of old trees.

A rare one here: only 5 on the map (#211 of 368 species).

Show Western larch trees on the map

Western larch, or "tamarack", is a people-eating monster in an Okanagan story, grabbing anyone who comes too close until Coyote strikes its top and declares that it will be used as medicine instead; that is why the tops of the tallest tamaracks are bent. Its sweet sap is a food, and a productive tree can give at least 4–5 litres twice a year (Spokan). In 2010 British Columbia began planting it about 1,000 km north of its range, the first assisted migration of a North American tree, to keep pace with a warming climate.

Indigenous uses from Nancy Turner, Ancient Pathways, Ancestral Knowledge (2014), vol. 1, p. 439; vol. 2, pp. 172, 261, 263–264.
